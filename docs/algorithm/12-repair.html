Stage 12 of 14

Untangling a boundary that crosses itself

Two stretches of a shape's outline, fitted correctly and independently, can still end up crossing each other where they pass close together. This stage finds every place that happens and redraws just enough of the boundary to stop it — without redrawing what was already right.

Technical reference: 12-repair.md

How a correct fit still goes wrong

The previous stage fits each stretch of a shape's outline on its own merits: does this curve pass close enough to the points it was measured against. That question is answered honestly and locally, one stretch at a time. Nothing in that question ever asks "does this curve also avoid crossing some other, entirely different stretch of the same outline drawn somewhere else."

Most of the time that omission costs nothing, because most of an outline does not come anywhere near itself. But where a shape narrows to a thin neck — two edges of the same outline running close and parallel for a stretch, the way the inside and outside of a comma or a thin bracket do — each edge is free to bulge very slightly toward the other while still fitting its own measured points perfectly. Fitted independently, the two bulges can simply meet in the middle and cross.

Why a crossing is worth fixing even though the picture looks fine

A drawing program fills a shape by a rule: walk around its outline and use the turns and crossings to decide what is inside and what is outside. That rule assumes the outline is a simple loop — one continuous line that does not cross itself. The moment it does cross itself, the fill rule still runs, but its answer for the small region cut off by the crossing is no longer the answer the shape intended. A letter's inner counter, or any other small hole in a shape, can end up painted solid when it should be empty, or empty when it should be solid, purely as a side effect of where the crossing happened to land.

The picture usually still looks nearly right, because the crossing is small and the area it flips is small. But "nearly right by accident" is a poor foundation for a document someone is going to open in an editor and try to work with. A shape that crosses itself is invalid regardless of how it happens to render, and it is the kind of thing that turns confusing the moment anyone tries to edit it by hand.

Not every crossing is a mistake. Where several parts of a drawing genuinely meet at a single point — a valid junction, not an accident of fitting — that is legitimate structure, and the repair is careful to leave it alone.

The fix: shrink the freedom, not the shape

The most direct way to guarantee no crossing is to stop giving the fit room to drift away from what was actually measured. Every measured point on a boundary, connected point to point with no smoothing at all, is guaranteed not to cross itself — it is exactly the outline the earlier stages traced, and that outline came from a real, single, non-overlapping shape in the first place.

So the repair does not try to detect and patch a crossing directly. It finds which stretches of the boundary are involved in one, and re-fits just those stretches under a shrinking limit on how far any one curve is allowed to span. Push that limit down far enough — eventually to "one curve per measured point" — and the crossing has nowhere left to hide, because the shape being redrawn is, at that point, exactly the original measurement. The repair only tightens the limit as far as it needs to on the parts that are actually guilty; everything else in the drawing is left exactly as the previous stage produced it.

Putting the good curve back where it is safe to

Tightening a curve's freedom to guarantee no crossing is a blunt tool: taken to its extreme it turns a smooth, compact curve back into a dense staircase of tiny segments — technically safe, but a worse drawing on every count that matters. So once the crossings are gone, the repair tries putting the original, more elegant fit back in place, and keeps it if doing so does not reopen the very crossing it just closed. Most of the time it can — the crossing usually only needed a small, local correction, not a wholesale rewrite of the whole stretch.

There is one deliberate exception. If tightening a stretch has already gone so far that it has exploded into hundreds of tiny segments — the staircase — that outcome is judged worse than simply keeping the earlier, more compact fit even if it still crosses. A measured comparison on a real, difficult case bore this out plainly: the smooth version that still crossed itself scored better against the original artwork, on every measure, than the technically-valid staircase that replaced it. A theoretical guarantee is not worth much if the fix it produces is worse than the problem.

Cost, kept in check

Redrawing a boundary at high detail is expensive to compute, not just visually heavy. An early version of this repair spent the overwhelming majority of one image's entire processing time re-tidying a single stubborn shape, because a costly smoothing pass was being re-run on every attempt, including the ones that were about to be discarded anyway. The fix was to run the expensive smoothing only once everything has settled and stopped crossing, and to give that final smoothing pass a firm budget — so a shape with an unusually large number of guilty stretches spends that budget on the shortest, cheapest fixes first, rather than exhausting it on one stretch and leaving the rest untouched.
