How a picture becomes a drawing

inkvec turns a grid of coloured squares into a set of shapes and curves. These pages explain how, one stage at a time — in plain language here, and in full technical detail in the reference documents beside them.

The problem

A photograph of a logo, or a screenshot of one, is a grid of pixels. Each pixel holds one colour. A designer's original file holds something quite different: a handful of shapes, each with an outline made of straight lines and smooth curves, each filled with a colour.

Going from the second to the first is easy — that is just drawing. Going back is the hard direction, and it is what inkvec does. The difficulty is that a great deal of information has been thrown away, and what is left is ambiguous: many different sets of shapes would produce exactly the same grid of pixels.

The idea that makes it work

Look closely at the edge of a shape on a screen and you will find pixels that are neither the shape's colour nor the background's, but something in between.

Most tracing tools treat those in-between pixels as a nuisance — blur to be cleaned up, a threshold to be picked. inkvec treats them as the most informative pixels in the image. A pixel that is 30% of the way from background to foreground is telling you that the shape covers 30% of it. That is a measurement, and a measurement narrows down where the edge runs to a fraction of a pixel — far finer than the pixel grid itself.

In one line

A blurry edge is not a problem to be removed. It is a ruler, and it is more precise than the grid it is drawn on.

This has a second consequence that matters just as much. Because the in-between pixels are measurements, inkvec can work out how confident it is about each one. Where two colours are strongly different the measurement is sharp; where they are nearly the same, it is vague — and the tool knows this, so it can afford to be approximate exactly where nobody could tell the difference.

The other idea: describing, not just matching

A tracer that only tried to match the pixels would produce a shape with thousands of tiny corners — a perfect match, and a useless drawing.

So inkvec is not asked to match the picture. It is asked to find the shortest honest description of it. Every curve it adds costs something; every bit of error it leaves also costs something; and it looks for the description where the total is smallest. Two flat colours may beat one gradient. One long curve may beat six short ones. The balance is a single number that runs through the whole system.

This is why the output tends to have roughly as many shapes as a person would have drawn, rather than thousands. It is also the mechanism behind most of the ways the tool can go wrong: if the picture has been through a compression step, the compression's own errors look like detail worth describing, and the tracer will faithfully describe them.

Every number, and where it came from

A system like this accumulates thresholds. Some are mathematical, some were measured against a corpus of over a thousand icons, and some were chosen once to fix one particular picture and never revisited.

constants.md lists all of them with their provenance, and marks the ones that look fitted to a single example rather than derived or measured. It exists to be uncomfortable reading.
