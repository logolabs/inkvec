Deciding how many colours a picture actually has

Not every distinct-looking pixel is a distinct ink, and not every pair of similar pixels is the same ink. This stage decides the palette by asking whether each candidate colour earns its keep — not by clustering pixels or picking a fixed distance and hoping it fits everywhere.

Technical reference: 03-palette.md

A question with two ways to get it wrong

Before anything about shapes or curves can be decided, the tracer needs to know: how many distinct colours — inks — did the artist actually use? This sounds simple and is not, because a picture never presents its palette cleanly. Every soft edge sprinkles in blend colours that are not really inks at all, and some genuinely different inks — two similar shades of pale pink, say — sit very close together in colour.

Get the count wrong in either direction and it shows:

Too few colours

Merge two inks the artist meant to keep separate, and the region loses detail — or worse, the tracer notices something is not quite flat there and invents a gradient to explain the leftover difference, when the truth was simply two flat colours side by side.

Too many colours

Mint an ink for every faint ripple a compressed file leaves near its edges, or for every step of an anti-aliased ramp, and a drawing with five real colours comes out with dozens — each one becoming its own shape, its own outline, its own path in the final file.

This is a genuine judgement call, not a lookup. It is what statisticians call a model selection problem — deciding how complex a description to use — rather than a clustering problem. Clustering asks "which pixels look similar." Model selection asks "does adding this colour to my description actually pay for itself, given how much of the picture it explains and how much noise there is to explain away."

Why a fixed ruler does not work

The obvious approach is to pick a colour space where distance roughly matches what a human eye perceives, and declare two colours "the same ink" whenever they sit within a fixed distance. This mostly works — until it meets the colour space's own quirks.

The space used here (OKLab) makes lightness a cube root of brightness, which is deliberate and mostly helpful — human vision is far more sensitive to differences among dark tones than among light ones, so building that sensitivity into the space keeps a single ruler roughly fair. But taken to its extreme, right down near black, that same cube root stretches distances enormously. A handful of the very darkest, almost imperceptible one-pixel-level differences near black can measure as farther apart, in this space, than two visibly different mid-tones. A clean black logo with no colour variation at all was, at one point, read as four separate near-black inks purely because of this stretch — differences nobody looking at the picture could ever see.

So the stage does not trust one ruler alone. It keeps a second, independent check — CIEDE2000, a colour-difference formula built specifically to match what people can actually distinguish — and uses it as a hard floor: below a small CIEDE2000 gap, two colours are declared the same ink no matter what any other test says, because no argument about "the artwork probably meant this" can rescue a difference nobody can see.

Does this colour earn its keep?

For every candidate colour, ordered from most to least common, the stage asks a version of the same question a scientist asks when deciding whether to add a new term to a model: does keeping this separate explain the picture enough better to be worth the extra complexity?

Concretely: if this candidate were folded into the nearest colour already accepted, how much error would that introduce across every pixel that would have chosen it? And is that error bigger than the fixed cost of writing one more colour into the file? A colour with thousands of pixels sitting well clear of anything already accepted almost always wins — the error saved by keeping it separate dwarfs the small fixed cost of adding it. A colour with a handful of pixels a hair's breadth from an existing one almost always loses — not worth a whole new entry for barely any benefit.

The comparison is scaled by how noisy the image is. In a very clean, exact render, even a small colour gap is trustworthy evidence, because there is nothing else that could have caused it. In an image known to have been resampled, upscaled or compressed, the same small gap is much less trustworthy — it could easily be an artefact — so the bar for keeping a nearby candidate separate is raised.

Telling a blend from a real pale colour

Every soft edge between two inks produces a ramp of in-between pixels — a coverage blend, not a colour choice. Left unchecked, that ramp would mint its own family of spurious inks. But a genuinely pale, real colour — a pastel pink, say — sits in exactly the same part of colour space as a blend between white and red. Colour position alone cannot tell the two apart. Shape can.

An anti-aliasing ramp is a thin band running along a boundary: almost none of its pixels have every neighbour also in the band, because the band is only a pixel or two wide. A real ink, even a small one, covers area — most of its pixels are safely surrounded by more of the same colour. This stage measures exactly that: what fraction of a candidate's pixels are "interior," meaning every neighbour also belongs to it.

There is one more wrinkle. A real ink drawn only two or three pixels wide is thin enough that it, too, has almost no fully-surrounded interior — the interior test alone cannot tell it apart from a ramp. For that narrow case, the stage asks one more question: does this colour's neighbourhood actually straddle the two colours it supposedly blends — having, close by, a neighbour clearly nearer one and a neighbour clearly nearer the other? A genuine coverage ramp always does. A real thin stroke of its own colour usually does not: it touches its neighbours on either side but is not caught between them. This distinction was needed after a real case where a stroke's shadow — a thin brown line that happened to be a mathematical mix of the shapes on either side of it — was nearly deleted as if it were only an anti-aliasing artefact.

Telling clean input from damaged input

How strict the palette should be depends on where the picture came from. An exact, cleanly rendered image can be trusted down to fine colour differences — if two nearby shades appear, the artist probably meant two shades. A picture that has been resized, blurred, or run through a compressed image format cannot be trusted the same way — its soft edges and its compression ripples both invent colours nobody chose.

This stage does not try to guess that from the colours alone; instead it is told, explicitly, by two upstream signals: how wide the picture's edges measure (a resized or blurred image has wider edges than a native render), and what file format the picture was saved in (some compressed formats are known to damage flat areas without widening edges at all — a case documented in the previous stage). Only when one of those signals says "this input is not clean" does the palette loosen its tolerance for near-duplicate colours and the confidence it grants to small colour gaps.

This has to stay conditional rather than always-on. Running the looser, more forgiving settings on a genuinely clean image was measured to cost real quality — about an eleven percent drop on a standard set of test icons — because on clean input a small colour gap really is the artist's choice, and blurring that distinction away throws artwork out along with the artefacts it was meant to catch.

What this stage hands forward

The result is a short list of colours — the recovered palette — plus, for each pixel in the picture, which of those colours it has been assigned to. Every later stage builds on top of this decision: the shapes that get outlined, the boundaries that get measured, and the gradients that get fitted are all defined in terms of these inks. Get the count right here, and everything downstream has the right raw material to work with.
